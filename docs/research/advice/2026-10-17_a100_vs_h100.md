# Advice: would an A100 make a worse agent than an H100-tailored run? (2026-10-17)

Advisory note (cloud advisor), requested by the owner through the local session. Not gate
evidence. Inputs: `2026-10-17_new_lane.md` (Lanes A and B), `2026-10-17_colab_review.md`,
`research/perf_quiet_20261013/RESULTS.md` (Mac and 4090 measurements, including the
precision and batch-scale rows), `learner_m3c.py` (`--batch-scale`, `--update-every`,
`--learner-precision`), `train_m3c.py` (`--all-snakes-learner`).

## Short answer

No. The GPU does not decide the agent's quality; the recipe does. The same recipe run on an
A100 and an H100 produces agents of the same quality up to run-to-run noise (CUDA float
non-determinism changes trajectories, not their distribution, and the gate runs on the Mac
anyway). An "H100-tailored" recipe is a different recipe (bigger batch, bigger teacher,
higher replay ratio, more members), and each of those knobs changes quality in its own
direction, up or down, regardless of the chip it runs on. In our setup an A100 can run
every one of those knobs too, at 1.2 to 1.5x the wall clock on the GPU-side part, which is
not the binding part. The only way slower becomes worse is through a budget cap: if credits
or session time force a shorter run or fewer members on the A100 than you would have afforded
on an H100. At Colab's rates the A100 gives more training per unit, so that cap bites the
H100 first, not the A100.

## Why the chip cannot make the agent worse here

- **The learner is tiny and launch-bound.** 1.5M parameters; an update took 8.4 ms on a 4090
  with CUDA graphs, dominated by kernel launches and the replay gather, not by FLOPs. An
  H100's advantage over an A100 is in FLOPs and memory bandwidth on large tensors; on this
  workload it is a minority of the step time. Expect 1.2 to 1.5x on the update, not 3x.
- **The binding resource is CPU actors.** Numba sim and featuriser on x86 vCPUs; the pods
  were actor-bound at about 4k transitions/s per run with the GPU idle. Self-play moves the
  per-snake forward onto the GPU, which is why it may clear 4x, but the sim steps stay on
  the CPU. Both Colab runtimes offer the same vCPU class. More GPU does nothing for them.
- **Quality enters only through what the learner computes per transition.** Replay ratio,
  batch size, network size, n-step and the loss are the quality-relevant quantities. All
  are software choices, all run on either GPU, and all must pass the same screen. The record
  already shows these knobs are not free: batch-scale 2 changed update semantics and needed
  its own A/B; fp16 and bf16 autocast were slower than fp32 on the 4090 because the network
  is too small for tensor-core tiers to pay, and lower precision in Q targets is a quality
  risk with no speed benefit here. FP8 is irrelevant for this size.
- **Per credit, the A100 is ahead.** At roughly 9 to 12 units per hour for the A100 and 13 for
  the H100, with the H100 maybe 1.3x faster on the GPU-side fraction of a mostly CPU-bound
  run, the A100 delivers more training transitions per unit. If anything constrains the
  recipe, it is total credits, and the A100 relaxes that constraint.

## Where an H100 could matter, and what would have to change to use it

An H100 only helps where the GPU is the bottleneck. The T16 smoke's utilization columns say
whether that ever happens; here is what it would take to make it happen on purpose.

1. **Raise compute per transition** (`--update-every` down or `--batch-scale` up with the
   lr-scale rule, and a replay ratio of 4 to 16 against today's one update per 128 ingested
   transitions). This is the BBF-style regime and it is Lane B's premise. It is a quality
   experiment first: it can overfit and destabilise without shrink-and-perturb style
   regularisation, so it enters as a pre-registered arm, not as an H100 default. Once the
   learner does 8 to 16 times more work per transition, the GPU can become the binding
   resource and an H100's 1.3 to 2x on a now-larger step is real.
2. **A larger teacher** (Lane B, 10 to 30M parameters, plus the own-body plane). At that size
   the update is compute-bound and the H100 is genuinely faster; the student stays 1.5M, so
   serving is unchanged. Again the question is whether the teacher is better, not whether the
   H100 trains it faster.
3. **Several league members' learners on one GPU.** Nothing stops 3 or 4 trainer processes
   sharing one A100 or H100; the limit is actors per member against the vCPU count. On a
   12-vCPU runtime that is at most 3 members with 3 actors each, whichever GPU you pick. An
   H100 helps only when those shared learners saturate the A100, which with 1.5M-parameter
   learners they will not.
4. **Keep fp32.** Do not turn on bf16 or fp16 for this network on either GPU; the 4090
   measurement showed it slower, and the compile path's fp32 verification is what guards
   against wrong gradients. TF32 matmul is already PyTorch's default for convolutions and is
   harmless here; leave it.
5. **CUDA graphs stay on** (`--compile-learner reduce-overhead`). This is the one setting that
   actually attacks the launch-bound update, and it works the same on both chips.

## What this means for the plan

- The smoke now running on an A100 answers the question that matters: does self-play clear
  4x, and is the GPU or the CPU the limit. Nothing in it should be rerun on an H100 unless the
  self-play rows show GPU utilization above about 70% with CPU below about 80%.
- Lane A (league) as written runs on an A100 with no change and no quality penalty. Its
  quality risks (style collapse, non-stationarity, boost drift) are recipe risks, not chip
  risks, and the Tier 1 guard and boost tripwires cover them identically.
- Lane B (large teacher, high replay ratio) is the only place an H100 earns its rate, and
  only after its first arm shows the teacher beats B8 on the six-snake product rosters. Run
  that first arm on the A100 at longer wall clock; move to the H100 for the follow-ups if the
  teacher wins and the learner is the bottleneck.
- Session limits are the real "slower becomes worse" risk on Colab, not the chip: a 24-hour
  cap or a disconnect truncates a run on either GPU. Checkpoint every few million
  transitions to Drive (not every 100k) and resume by weights, as the smoke already does, and
  size each member's run to finish inside one session with margin.
- Notebook: no change is needed for an H100 beyond selecting the runtime; the pins, the
  torch build and the sampler are architecture-agnostic. If the smoke's result were later
  compared across chips, both runs would need the same run name convention and the same
  commit, which the identity check already enforces.

## One line

An A100 trains the same recipe to the same agent, slower on the part that is not the
bottleneck; the H100 only pays once a pre-registered recipe deliberately makes the learner the
bottleneck, and that recipe has to earn its place on quality first.
